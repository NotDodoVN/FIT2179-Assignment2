Australia's Greenhouse Gas Emissions

Australia is one of the world's highest per-capita emitters. Decades of fossil fuel dependence across energy, agriculture and transport have loaded the atmosphere with heat-trapping gases — setting every downstream crisis in motion.

Chapter 03 — The Burning Continent

Wildfire Intensity & Frequency

Australia's bushfire seasons are growing longer, hotter, and more destructive. The 2019–20 "Black Summer" became a global symbol of climate-driven catastrophe — and it is increasingly the norm, not the exception.

MODIS satellite fire detection for H1 2026 — brightness temperature indicates fire intensity. A substantial number of wildfire events are evident within this period, with occurrences concentrated primarily along the coastal regions of several states. Victoria exhibits the highest frequency of wildfire activity, while South Australia records the lowest. The proportional breakdown of wildfire occurrences can be examined more clearly in Chart 6.

This polar chart presents the distribution of bushfire incidents by state across two six‑year periods (2011–2016 and 2016–2021). Across both time intervals, Queensland and the Northern Territory record the highest frequencies of bushfire activity, reflecting their extensive land areas, vegetation characteristics, and climatic conditions that are conducive to fire occurrence.

Chapter 05 — The Shrinking Harvest

Crop Production Under Pressure

The final link in the chain. Heat stress, water scarcity, and bushfire smoke combine to reduce Australia's crop yields — threatening domestic food security and export capacity.

Across all four monitored crops, the 2024–25 season reflects comparatively modest production volumes against the 2025–26 projections. Wheat, while remaining the dominant commodity at approximately 3,450 kt, falls short of the projected 4,050 kt, and Barley similarly records a lower output of 2,000 kt relative to the following season's projected 2,550 kt. Canola and Lentils likewise post constrained figures of 925 kt and 500 kt respectively — the latter representing the weakest performance across both periods. These 2024–25 outcomes suggest that crop yields remained under measurable pressure during this period, consistent with the broader climate-driven stressors of heat, water scarcity, and fire risk that characterised the season.

Victoria’s harvest is on track for a strong year. Production is expected to climb 17%, reaching 9.1 million tonnes in 2025–26, a noticeable lift from earlier expectations. It’s now sitting 3% higher than the September 2025 Australian Crop Report and 11% above the 10‑year average. Much of this momentum comes from the well‑timed October rains, which arrived just when crops needed them most, setting up healthier yields and giving later‑sown paddocks across the state a welcome boost.

The convergence of heat, fire, and water stress is compressing crop yields in ways no single stressor could achieve alone. Australia's agricultural sector — which feeds millions domestically and exports globally — faces a structural challenge. Addressing it requires tracing the chain all the way back to its source: emissions.
